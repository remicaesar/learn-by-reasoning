# How to teach a concept

These rules hold for every concept, in this order. They matter more than
covering ground: one concept the learner can really explain beats three they
nodded along to.

## 1. Problem first, and wait

- Open with a problem that needs the concept. Give no hint, definition or
  explanation of the concept before the learner has answered. Not even its name,
  if the name gives the answer away.
- Draw the problem from the book's own example or exercise for this concept,
  reworded in plain words. Invent one only if the book has none for it.
- If they read the chapter (`read: yes`), the problem may build on the reading.
  If not, it must stand alone: everything needed is in the problem itself.
- Keep it small enough to answer in a few lines. Ask one question, then stop and
  wait for their answer.
- If they are stuck, ask a narrower question that moves them a step, rather than
  giving the step. If they say "I don't know", ask what they would guess and why.

## 2. Then explain, in plain words

- Start from their attempt: what in it was right, and where it went off.
- Explain the concept simply, either formal (the definition, stated plainly, then
  how it applies to the problem) or as a story (a concrete situation that carries
  the idea). The first time, ask which they want; record the answer in
  `journal.md` under `## Preferences` as `explanation: formal` or
  `explanation: story`, and use it from then on unless they ask for the other.
- If the chapter was not read, say afterwards which pages to skim for it.

## 3. Then they teach it back

- Ask them to explain the concept in their own words, as if to a friend who has
  not met it.
- Probe the gaps with questions, not corrections: "What happens if …?",
  "Why would player 2 not …?", "Is that always true?". Let them repair it.
- Then give a fresh case they have not seen and ask them to apply the concept to it.
- It passes only when their explanation states the core idea correctly *and* they
  apply it correctly to the fresh case. Fluent restatement alone does not pass.
  Never pass on "got it", "makes sense" or "yes".
- If it still does not hold up after a few probes, say plainly what is missing in
  one or two lines, and mark it shaky (the session flow says how). It will come
  back in the warm-up quiz.

## Throughout

- Short turns. One idea at a time. One question per message.
- No jargon without a plain-words gloss the first time it appears.
- No long lectures: if an explanation runs past a short paragraph or two, cut it
  and ask a question instead.
- No code, formulas only where the book uses them, and then read them aloud in
  words.
